Case study · Brainfish + Ballet

Designing the boundary between human intent and machine autonomy.

I designed two AI-native products from scratch, moving from knowledge and answering to trusted agentic action for support and operations teams.

Role: Product Design Lead Scope: 0→1 AI product design Focus: Trust, control, agentic systems design, agentic workflows
01 · Context

The problem changed as AI gained agency.

Brainfish started by helping people find and understand company knowledge. As customers asked AI to do more, the design challenge shifted from answering accurately to acting predictably.

01
Answer Retrieve and synthesize trusted knowledge.
02
Recommend Identify knowledge improvements and propose changes.
03
Initiate Recognise when information is not enough and trigger a procedure.
04
Execute Run complex, connected workflows with bounded AI autonomy.
The moment AI moves from answering to acting, the design problem changes from relevance to trust.
02 · Brainfish

An AI-native support platform with two core jobs.

Brainfish helps mid-market and enterprise teams manage company knowledge and deploy AI answering experiences across help centres, chat, Slack, Microsoft Teams and API-powered surfaces.

Manage knowledge

Self-healing knowledge

Customers centralise content and connect external sources. The Content Management Agent analyses the knowledge base and surfaces Knowledge Discoveries for review and approval.

Deploy experiences

Answer & act anywhere

The Answering Agent powers multiple channels. I designed the conversational experience and the control plane that defines knowledge, behaviour, style and custom instructions.

What we learned

Customers said they wanted autopilot. In practice, they wanted control.

As AI became more capable, trust came less from hiding complexity and more from giving users the right visibility and authority at the right moment.

Design principle
Deterministic where reliability matters. Agentic where judgement adds value.
03 · Ballet

Turning ambiguous intent into executable workflows.

Ballet lets non-technical teams create complex support and operations workflows. AI helps plan and build the workflow, while the execution model remains explicit and inspectable.

You can use a Ballet workflow on it's own within the product, or you can reference a workflow in Brainfish within a Procedure.

1. Planning Agent Conversation helps a knowledge worker move from a desired outcome to a well-structured workflow.
2. Human review The user validates intent before the system is given execution autonomy.
3. Builder Agent The reviewed plan becomes coded, deterministic workflow steps.
4. Agents + Tools Agents are added only where judgement or autonomous action is valuable. Tools connect steps to external systems and data.
5. Test + inspect Users can run real, mock or manual inputs, inspect every step, and validate inputs and outputs before deployment.
6. Deploy + recover Workflows run manually or via webhooks. When a step fails, the Builder Agent helps diagnose and repair the issue.
04 · Interaction model

Conversation is useful when the problem is still ambiguous.

Most users told us they already knew the workflow they wanted to build. In practice, they often knew the outcome, but struggled to describe the underlying process.

The Planning Agent turns that ambiguity into structure, then hands a reviewed plan to the Builder Agent.

Planning Agent

Ambiguity → intent

Clarifies the goal, required systems, conditions, data and expected outcomes through conversation.

Builder Agent

Intent → implementation

Creates deterministic steps, connects tools, configures agents and prepares the workflow for test and execution.

05 · Transparency

Power without technical overload.

Traditional node-based workflow builders exposed too much implementation detail. Even when users tolerated some technical complexity, they quickly became confused and abandoned the experience.

Level 1

Outcome

What will happen? Human-readable steps make the workflow understandable at a glance.

Level 2

Logic

How will it happen? Users can inspect conditions, inputs, outputs and relationships between steps.

Level 3

Intelligence

What is the agent actually doing? Reveal context, tools and behaviour when deeper understanding is required.

We stopped treating transparency as “show everything” and designed for progressive technical transparency instead.
06 · Trust

Make the system inspectable before asking users to trust it.

Users can test workflows with real data, mock data or manual inputs, then inspect a run audit for every step, including its inputs and outputs.

01
Build Create the workflow through Planning and Builder Agents.
02
Test Use controlled data before deployment.
03
Inspect Understand what happened at every step.
04
Refine Repair or adjust before giving the workflow more autonomy.
07 · Recovery

Failure is part of the experience.

Technical accuracy isn’t the same as useful system feedback.

When a workflow fails, Ballet surfaces system status and gives the user a path to ask the Builder Agent to diagnose what happened and attempt a repair.

A trustworthy agentic product needs to help users recover, not just explain why the system failed.
08 · Live learning

Designing toward self-service through real operational complexity.

Ballet is live with a small number of customers today. We currently use an FDE model to help build workflows, which gives me direct feedback from the people using the product in real customer contexts.

The next threshold is PLG: not whether Ballet can build complex workflows, but whether someone without an FDE can understand, trust, repair and deploy one.

Internal analytics

Measure the creation experience

We monitor time-to-build, context drop-out, workflow creation and completion to understand where the experience breaks down.

Customer analytics

Make workflow outcomes visible

An Analytics Agent is being designed to generate workflow-specific dashboards based on steps, runs and connected systems.

09 · Learning

Three assumptions changed the product direction.

Autopilot

Automation did not equal trust.

Customers valued AI recommendations, but often wanted to review and approve what became authoritative knowledge.

Signal

More intelligence created more noise.

Computer Vision helped surface Knowledge Discoveries, but generated too much noise when customers were deciding which sessions should influence their knowledge.

Control

Flexible instructions were not predictable enough.

Custom Instructions gave customers freedom, but many did not know what to write or still wanted stronger behavioural control. That learning helped lead to Procedures.

10 · Principles

What I learned designing agentic systems.

01
Autonomy must be earned through trust.Increase autonomy as users gain evidence that the system behaves predictably.
02
Use conversation to structure ambiguity.Chat is useful when neither the user nor the system has enough structure yet.
03
Establish intent before execution.Separate planning from building, and give humans a clear review boundary.
04
Keep consequential work deterministic.Use Agents selectively where judgement adds value, not because every step can be agentic.
05
Reveal complexity progressively.Start with what will happen, then expose how and why when users need deeper control.
06
Make AI inspectable.Users need to understand what happened, what data was used and what each Agent actually did.
07
Design recovery as deliberately as success.Failure handling, diagnosis and repair are core parts of autonomous products.
08
Expert-assisted success does not equal self-service success.The standard changes when a customer has to understand and operate the system alone.
Why this matters for Atticus

Different domain. Similar design problem.

My work has focused on designing the boundary between human intent and machine autonomy: how people establish what they want, how AI translates that intent into action, and how those actions remain understandable, controllable and trustworthy.

That same tension is what makes high-stakes, evidence-driven AI products compelling to me.
